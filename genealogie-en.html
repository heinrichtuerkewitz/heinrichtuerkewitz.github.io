A Critique of First Mentions

In genealogical research, especially with noble families, one is often confronted with the first mention, which frequently gives an impressively early date together with a document. The Gotha (Gothaischer Hofkalender, from 1763, in French Almanach de Gotha until 1944), the Genealogisches Handbuch des Adels (GHdA, 1951 to 2015, 158 volumes) as its successor, the Adelslexikon published within it (1972 to 2012, 18 volumes), and the Deutsches Geschlechterbuch as a handbook of bourgeois families rank among the standard works, alongside numerous other genealogical reference works. Some caution is nevertheless called for with regard to the first mentions of families given there and in all other relevant works published to date. For many families the first documentary mention may well correlate with the later Stammreihe, but in numerous cases the relationship between the two must be judged far more carefully.

This applies in particular to families whose names are not marked by rarity. The first mention, which many reference works attribute to the family and not merely to its name, or at least give that impression, often lies several decades or even centuries before the beginning of the Stammreihe documented without gaps.

While such an attribution may be correct in an individual case, in most cases it is an expression of a certain wishful thinking and of the desire for the longest possible chain of ancestors, which was often adopted uncritically. If for this early period there is no proof of a coat of arms or other evidence suggesting actual membership of the family, the connection often rests solely on geographical proximity and a similar-sounding name. For some families the possession of an ancestral seat may speak for membership, but even this does not always offer final certainty. Given the lack of continuity of names in the Middle Ages, a first mention can hardly close a gap of a hundred years or more. It then often does not even offer a secure assumption of membership but remains an unfounded and unsubstantiated speculation.

The Silesian Uradel family Troschke may serve as an example here: according to the Adelslexikon it first appears in documents in 1311, and its secured Stammreihe begins with a v. Troschke (documented in 1442). The same applies to the ancient noble Trotha of the Saalkreis, who first appear in 1291 with Willibertus et Tilo de Trote and whose Stammreihe begins with Nikolaus (documented 1376–1412). A further example is the Thuringian Uradel family Wechmar, which first appears in documents with Sinoldus de Wemchar in 1170, while its secured Stammreihe begins only 230 years later, in 1400, with Reinhard v. Wechmar. This list could probably be continued indefinitely.

Most often this first documentary mention concerns Uradel families, but also houses ennobled by letters patent (Briefadel), such as the famous Austrian industrial family of the Freiherren Mayr v. Melnhof. It is first mentioned in documents in 1434, whereas the secured Stammreihe begins only in 1750, some 320 years later. While this may still be somewhat plausible in this case, since a conclusion might possibly be drawn from the family’s farms and possessions, it is nevertheless questionable, not to mention the many further cases that cannot all be listed individually here, in which neither the name, which is often not all that rare, nor the ancestral seat establishes a connection that can be reliably proven, or even necessarily strongly presumed.

In older reference works this is easier to understand, since, especially in the later 18th century but also well beyond the late 19th century, they were often marked by wishful thinking and embellishment. Yet even today some caution is called for with regard to every work. In case of doubt, one should always fall back on contemporary primary sources, insofar as they still exist.

These remarks are by no means directed against the genealogical handbooks, which rightly remain standard works to this day. They are rather intended to foster a critical attitude in research. Information on the first documentary mention of a family, provided it is really well founded, is certainly of great historical interest. All too often, however, such claims are rather far-fetched when it comes to attributing them with certainty to the particular family. Once put into circulation, such errors often ran through almost all works and could only be laboriously refuted or corrected through my own primary research.
