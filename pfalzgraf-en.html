Pfalzgraf and Hofpfalzgraf (Count Palatine and Court Count Palatine)

Anyone who deals with historical coats of arms and their grant will without doubt encounter the term Hofpfalzgraf (court count palatine) and will also ask about the difference from the Pfalzgraf (count palatine), such as the Count Palatine of Bavaria, among others. For this reason these two terms are explained here once more in brief, also drawing on various previously published works and websites.

The Pfalzgrafen (Counts Palatine)

The term “Pfalzgraf” goes back to the Latin comes palatinus, the count of the palace. It referred to a man from the immediate circle of the ruler. This office originated as early as the Merovingian period, in the course of the 6th century. Under the Carolingians the office grew into the highest authority in secular matters at court and presided over the palace court. The office was thus at first primarily a task, not a title, and was connected with the administration of royal estates.

From the later 10th century the position and character of this office changed. In the duchies of Saxony, Bavaria, Swabia and Lorraine, counts palatine emerged who served the king as a counterweight to the powerful dukes. They held the second rank in the duchy and also exercised jurisdiction in place of the ruler. Most of these counties palatine did not last long or passed to other families. This was the case, for example, with the Bavarian office, which already died out with Rapoto III of Ortenburg in 1248.

Only the county palatine that emerged from the Lotharingian county palatine, the Pfalzgrafschaft bei Rhein (Count Palatine of the Rhine), remained in existence. From 1214 its holders always came from the House of Wittelsbach. With the Golden Bull of 1356 the electoral dignity was finally secured. As imperial vicar, the Count Palatine of the Rhine conducted the king’s affairs during a vacancy of the throne. The electoral dignity overlay the title of count palatine, and “Pfalz” (Palatinate) became the name of the land itself. This change of meaning explains why “Pfalz” today means a territory and no longer a royal court. After the Napoleonic era parts of the old Palatinate returned to Bavaria, and the title “Pfalzgraf bei Rhein” remained part of the titles of the Bavarian kings until 1918.

Pfalzgraf and Hofpfalzgraf

The two terms must be clearly separated. The Pfalzgraf was a holder of sovereign power. The later Hofpfalzgraf, by contrast, had no territorial rule, only personally granted powers. Whoever comes across “Pfalzgraf” in a source must therefore first clarify which of the two groups is meant. The confusion is frequent in older literature.

Hofpfalzgrafen (Court Counts Palatine)

The Hofpfalzgraf (Latin comes palatinus caesareus) also bears the name of the medieval count palatine but is clearly to be distinguished from him and may therefore often cause confusion. The Pfalzgraf was, as already explained in detail, a holder of sovereign power with judicial authority and a territory of his own, from which, as in the case of the Palatinate of the Rhine, an electorate emerged. The Hofpfalzgraf, to be distinguished from him and often considerably more relevant for private family research, had no such claim by virtue of his office. His position rested solely on a personally granted privilege.

This office was granted by the Emperor or the Pope. Hofpfalzgrafen were by no means rare; already in the 16th century their number had risen to over a hundred.

At first the so-called Palatinat covered everyday legal acts, for example the appointment of notaries, the legitimation of illegitimate children, declarations of majority and the freeing of unfree persons. Later, from about the middle of the 16th century, it regularly covered the granting of coats of arms to commoners; more rarely, only where the wording of the privilege provided for it, the power to ennoble was also included. The extent of the rights was never entirely uniform and therefore often results solely from the respective charter of grant.

Greater and lesser Palatinat

A distinction was made between the greater and the lesser Palatinat, although this distinction cannot always be drawn clearly in research. The widespread assumption that the greater Palatinat entitled its holder to ennoble is refuted by charters that granted a greater Palatinat without this right. Today the greater Palatinat is therefore predominantly defined by the fact that its holder was in turn allowed to appoint Hofpfalzgrafen. The Palatinat could be granted personally (ad personam) or hereditarily. Individual universities, too, received a Palatinat, among other things, and were thus allowed to grant coats of arms.

The Hofpfalzgraf as grantor of arms

Coats of arms were originally granted by feudal lords to their vassals, later also by the Emperor, and from about 1500 increasingly by the Hofpfalzgrafen. Legally this was an act of grace, which in practice was an administrative procedure paid for with high fees. Letters of arms resemble letters of nobility outwardly but do not establish nobility. Often the inability to decipher the writing of the time may have led to the false conclusion that many a family laid claim to nobility.

It may also seem interesting that the designation “Hofpfalzgraf” may at first suggest that the recipient was already noble or in any case received nobility by virtue of the grant of the office. This was not the case, however: nobility was never a prerequisite for receiving the Palatinat, nor did the recipient thereby obtain noble status (no office nobility). Bourgeois notaries or notables could also become Hofpfalzgrafen and made up a large share of them. This circumstance may be confusing at first sight, since a bourgeois Hofpfalzgraf could thus grant both coats of arms and, if he even held the greater Palatinat, nobility.

It is therefore necessary to check in the respective charter which privilege the issuer relied on, whether the wording covers the power and whether the charter exists as an original, as a copy or only in the literature. A common reference work for this is the Hofpfalzgrafen-Register by Jürgen Arndt.
