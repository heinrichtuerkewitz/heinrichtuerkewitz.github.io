Pfalzgraf and Hofpfalzgrafen (Counts Palatine and Court Counts Palatine)

Anyone who deals with historical coats of arms and their grant will without doubt encounter the term Hofpfalzgraf (court count palatine) and will also ask about the difference from the Pfalzgraf (count palatine), such as the Count Palatine of Bavaria, among others. For this reason these two terms are explained here once more in brief, also drawing on various previously published works and websites.

The Pfalzgrafen (Counts Palatine)

The term “Pfalzgraf” goes back to the Latin comes palatinus, the count of the palace. It referred to a man from the immediate circle of the ruler. This office originated as early as the Merovingian period, in the course of the 6th century. Under the Carolingians the office grew into the highest authority in secular matters at court and presided over the palace court. The office was thus at first primarily an administrative task, not a title.

From the later 10th century the position and character of this office changed. In the duchies of Saxony, Bavaria, Swabia and Lorraine, counts palatine emerged who served the king as a counterweight to the powerful dukes. They held the second rank in the duchy and also exercised jurisdiction in place of the ruler. Most of these counties palatine did not last long or passed to other families. This was the case, for example, with the Bavarian office, which already died out with Rapoto III of Ortenburg in 1248.

Only the county palatine that emerged from the Lotharingian county palatine, the Pfalzgrafschaft bei Rhein (Count Palatine of the Rhine), remained in existence. From 1214 its holders always came from the House of Wittelsbach. With the Golden Bull of 1356 the electoral dignity was finally secured. As imperial vicar, the Count Palatine of the Rhine conducted the king’s affairs during a vacancy of the throne. The electoral dignity overlay the title of count palatine, and “Pfalz” (Palatinate) became the name of the land itself. This change of meaning explains why “Pfalz” today means a territory and no longer a royal court. After the Napoleonic era parts of the old Palatinate returned to Bavaria, and the title “Pfalzgraf bei Rhein” remained part of the titles of the Bavarian kings until 1918.

Pfalzgraf and Hofpfalzgraf

The two terms must be clearly separated. The Pfalzgraf was a holder of sovereign power. The later Hofpfalzgraf, by contrast, had no territorial rule, only powers granted personally (ad personam) or hereditarily.

The Palatinat of the Hofpfalzgraf

For private family research the Hofpfalzgraf (Latin comes palatinus caesareus) is usually more important than the Pfalzgraf, for it was he who in the 16th and 17th centuries – in the end, however, much reduced – until the end of the Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation in 1806 granted coats of arms to many bourgeois families. His Palatinat, that is, the extent of his rights, was granted by the Emperor or the Pope personally (ad personam) or hereditarily. Already in the 16th century there were over a hundred Hofpfalzgrafen.

The actual extent of a given grant can usually be gathered only from the respective charter of grant. It mostly concerned simple legal acts of everyday life, such as the appointment of notaries or the legitimation of illegitimate children, up to the granting of coats of arms to commoners, which became customary from about the middle of the 16th century. The right to ennoble existed only where the charter expressly granted it.

A distinction is made between the so-called greater and lesser Palatinat, although the boundary does not appear entirely clear. Today the mark of the greater Palatinat is that its holder was himself allowed to appoint further Hofpfalzgrafen. The older assumption that it always also entitled its holder to ennoble can be refuted by charters that granted a greater Palatinat even without a right of ennoblement. In isolated cases certain universities were also granted a Palatinat and consequently were allowed to grant coats of arms.

The Hofpfalzgraf as grantor of coats of arms

Coats of arms were originally granted by a feudal lord to his vassals (with the exception of the so-called Uradel, which mostly adopted its arms itself at uncertain times), later also by the Emperor, and from about 1500 increasingly by the Hofpfalzgrafen. Legally this was an act of grace, which in practice was an administrative procedure paid for with high fees and was also meant to improve the state coffers, so that in the course of time a veritable business was made of grants of arms and ennoblements. Letters of arms resemble letters of nobility outwardly but do not establish nobility. Often the inability to decipher the writing of the time may have led to the false conclusion that many a family laid claim to nobility.

It may also seem interesting that the designation “Hofpfalzgraf” may at first suggest that the recipient was already noble or in any case received nobility by virtue of the grant of the office. This was not the case, however: nobility was never a prerequisite for receiving the Palatinat, nor did the recipient thereby obtain noble status (no office nobility). Bourgeois notaries or notables could also become Hofpfalzgrafen and made up a large share of them. This circumstance may be confusing at first sight, since a bourgeois Hofpfalzgraf could thus grant both coats of arms and, if he even held the greater Palatinat, nobility.

It is therefore necessary to check in the respective charter which privilege the issuer relied on, whether the wording covers the power and whether the charter exists as an original, as a copy or only in the literature. A common reference work for this is the Hofpfalzgrafen-Register by Jürgen Arndt.
