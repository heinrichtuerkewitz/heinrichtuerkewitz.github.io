Coat-of-Arms Fraud and Usurpations

Coats of arms often provide great help, particularly in genealogy, without which many attributions to families would be impossible. But they also carry the danger of suggesting false conclusions. Especially since the beginning of the 19th century, the rising middle class was interested in its family-historical roots and often also in embellishing them.

In the course of the century this became an extremely popular pursuit. The ordinary citizen often pursued it merely out of interest in the past and history of his ancestors, but many rising industrialists, businessmen and the like sought in past generations a legitimation for their newly acquired social standing. Through this development, hundreds of genealogical bureaus soon sprang up all over Europe, all intended to research the family backgrounds of paying customers. Not a few of them were hardly reputable, and so already toward the end of the 19th century a number of forgeries came to light that aimed at so-called renobilitations, that is, at regaining the old, supposedly forgotten noble status. Many other bureaus as well, both local and itinerant, travelled across Europe and palmed off on unsuspecting persons coats of arms of the same name that had supposedly been granted, or even entirely invented ones. More on this in the further articles at the end of this page.

Since this topic is described sufficiently and in great detail on other websites, I see little point in doing so again here. But since it also often led to questions in my own research, and a coat of arms handed down in the family is probably often the trigger for the search for one’s own genealogy, it cannot be passed over entirely on this website. It is only meant to remind readers once more to be extremely critical, especially in this field.

In many cases an “old” inherited coat of arms, said to have stood in the family for a hundred years, is accepted in good faith as genuine. Often this is combined with the story of an old but forgotten or abandoned nobility. “We were probably once nobility …” – how often have I heard these words in the course of my research, also from acquaintances and friends! While in a tiny fraction of cases this may actually be true – there are several examples from history proving that a family had in fact forgotten its nobility, supposedly “lasting for all time” – in most cases it is fabricated and traceable to coat-of-arms forgers and swindlers of the 19th century.

Most of the families affected are unaware of these facts for lack of knowledge, and little can be held against them in their good faith. At the slightest indication, however, the matter should be examined promptly and, if the result is adverse, the wrongly borne coat of arms should be laid down again. This path, however, leads only through a careful examination of the genealogy back to an ancestor who actually bore arms. In some cases the extent of the forgery is so absurd, or so easy for an expert to detect, that examining the inherited depiction of the coat of arms seems the most sensible first step. There are also cases in which grand coats of arms with ermine mantles and princely hats or even royal crowns were issued to bourgeois families.

A statement that is made again and again, and is correct in this form, is that historically not only the nobility but also bourgeois families were entitled to bear arms (wappenfähig) and did bear arms. This should not, however, lead to the assumption that nearly all of them actually did. The majority of the old bourgeois strata bore no coat of arms of their own. Many merchants, craftsmen and the like were indeed obliged to seal documents and therefore often used guild marks, initials and the like, which were richly decorated; but one must not make the mistake of misunderstanding these as coats of arms.

Families of lower social standing as a rule bore no coat of arms. There are, however, quite a few documented craftsman families and in part even peasant families that did indeed bear arms. Nor should one believe that coats of arms necessarily had to be granted. While this was the case for many, the greater part of the surviving coats of arms goes back to their adoption without an official grant by a sovereign or a count palatine.

In conclusion, this is meant only to encourage people to always subject their own or familiar coats of arms to careful examination and not to believe all family myths and legends without checking, even if these can in part rest on true events.
