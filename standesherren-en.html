Standesherren: Concept and History

Anyone confronted with the so-called “high nobility” in their genealogical research will also be familiar with the red volume of the Genealogisches Handbuch der Fürstlichen Häuser (Genealogical Handbook of the Princely Houses) and its three categories, which can be somewhat confusing at first. The first division seems clear: it comprises the few European princely houses that still reign today (see Sweden, Great Britain, etc.) and those that were dethroned since the beginning of the 19th century. The second division, which I will come to in more detail below, concerns the so-called “Standesherrliche Häuser” (houses of the Standesherren): formerly imperial-estate, later standesherrlich subordinate princely and comital houses with the right of equality of birth (Ebenbürtigkeit) with the ruling princes (1st division). The third division concerns the princely houses that never enjoyed any form of sovereignty and are thus purely titular princes.

The term Standesherren caused me considerable confusion on my first contacts with it, so I would like to set it out briefly together with its history.

On 6 August 1806 Francis II/I laid down the imperial crown, and with that the Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation became history. At that time the Empire comprised 266 territories with imperial membership and full territorial sovereignty: 72 ecclesiastical, 143 secular and 51 Free Imperial Cities. Many of these had already disappeared by then. The Reichsdeputationshauptschluss of 1803 abolished 112 imperial estates on the right bank of the Rhine alone, and the founding of the Confederation of the Rhine cost some 70 further lordships their independence. Whoever did not perish was mediatised, that is, placed under the territorial sovereignty of a sovereign state. The Congress of Vienna also brought further mediatisations.

Standesherren, or the mediatised, are therefore those princes and counts who lost their imperial immediacy and territorial sovereignty between 1803 and 1815. They are, in a sense, a product of German particularism (Kleinstaaterei). The term itself first appears in Article XIV of the German Federal Act of 8 June 1815. It secured for the mediatised their membership in the high nobility and their equality of birth with the ruling houses. In 1825 (princes) and 1829 (counts) the federal states reported their standesherrlich houses to the Federal Assembly, which finally settled who counted as a Standesherr and who did not. As a style, these houses were further granted “Durchlaucht” (Serene Highness) for the heads of the princely houses and “Erlaucht” (Illustrious Highness) for those of the comital houses.

To understand who belonged to the high nobility at all, one must look at the Imperial Diet. It met permanently in Regensburg from 1663 and consisted of three colleges: the Electoral College, the Council of Princes and the Council of Imperial Cities. High nobility were those who had a seat and vote in the Diet. In the Council of Princes, however, sat not only princes but also counts and barons. While the secular princes each held a vote of their own (Virilstimme), the counts, around 1790 no fewer than 124 in number, had to join together in the four Counts’ Benches. Each bench had only a single collective vote (Kuriatstimme).

The prerequisite for this was, in principle, land of one’s own that was recorded in the imperial register (Reichsmatrikel) and was subject only to the Emperor. Whoever possessed such land was called a Realist and ruled his territory himself, even if in many cases it was geographically hardly significant, that is, small. Personalists, by contrast, had a seat and vote but no such land. They were at first admitted to a college on probation, with the expectation that they would acquire suitable property within a foreseeable time. Their position was tied to the person and could at first end with his death. Through the acquisition or sale of such lordships a family repeatedly moved between high and lower nobility.

For recognition as a Standesherr, finally, a single cut-off date counted: 6 August 1806. Only those who held an individual or collective vote on that day were later regarded as mediatised. If a family sold its formerly immediate estates only afterwards, this changed nothing about its status. This applied, for example, to the Auersperg, Colloredo, Lobkowicz, Metternich, Stadion and Trauttmansdorff-Weinsberg. Conversely, one could no longer become a Standesherr by an acquisition after that date.

The legal final line was drawn by the First World War. The Weimar Constitution of 14 August 1919 abolished the nobility as a class, and titles have counted since then only as part of the name. Since then there has been no nobility in the legal sense.

The interests of these mediatised were represented from April 1864 by the “Verein der Standesherren”, with Carl Egon, Prince of Fürstenberg, as its first president. This association exists to this day as a private-law association of the German Standesherren.

The standesherrlich families still existing today include the Arenberg, Auersperg-Trautson, Bentheim und Steinfurt, Bentheim-Tecklenburg, Castell-Castell, Castell-Rüdenhausen, Colloredo-Mannsfeld, Croy, Erbach-Erbach, Erbach-Fürstenau, Esterhazy von Galantha, Fugger von Babenhausen, Fugger von Glött, Fürstenberg, Harrach, Hohenlohe-Bartenstein, Hohenlohe-Langenburg, Hohenlohe-Oehringen, Hohenlohe-Waldenburg, Isenburg, Khevenhüller-Metsch, Königsegg-Aulendorf, Kuefstein, Leiningen, Lobkowicz, Löwenstein-Wertheim-Freudenberg, Löwenstein-Wertheim-Rosenberg, Neipperg, Oettingen-Oettingen, Oettingen-Wallerstein, Orsini und Rosenberg, Ortenburg, Pappenheim, Platen-Hallermund, Quadt zu Wykradt und Isny, Ratibor, Rechberg und Rothenlöwen, Rechteren-Limpurg, Salm-Horstmar, Salm-Salm, Sayn-Wittgenstein-Berleburg, Sayn-Wittgenstein-Hohenstein, Sayn-Wittgenstein-Sayn, Schaesberg, Schönborn-Buchheim, Schönborn-Wiesentheid, Schönburg-Hartenstein, Schwarzenberg, Solms-Baruth, Solms-Hohensolms-Lich, Solms-Laubach, Solms-Rödelheim und Assenheim, Starhemberg, Stolberg-Stolberg, Stolberg-Wernigerode, Thurn und Taxis, Toerring-Jettenbach, Trauttmansdorff-Weinsberg, Waldburg zu Wolfegg, Waldburg zu Zeil, Wied, Windisch-Graetz and Ysenburg und Büdingen.

Not all houses that counted among the Standesherren in 1806 are still represented on this list. Some became extinct in the course of the 19th and 20th centuries, among them the Kaunitz-Rietberg, the Dietrichstein, the Stadion and the Metternich, to name just a few examples.
